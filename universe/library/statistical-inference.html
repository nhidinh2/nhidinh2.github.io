Statistical inference

Inference turns observations into statements about parameters, predictions, or decisions— while being explicit about assumptions, variability, and what could go wrong if the model is misspecified. We treat the data as a sample $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ from a model $\{f(x; \theta) : \theta \in \Theta\}$ and ask what the sample says about the unknown $\theta$.

Point estimation

An estimator $\hat\theta = T(X_1, \ldots, X_n)$ is any statistic used to guess $\theta$. Two general recipes dominate.

  • Method of moments: equate the first $k$ sample moments to the population moments and solve. Set $\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_i X_i^{\,j} = E_\theta[X^j]$ for $j = 1, \ldots, k$, then invert for $\hat\theta$. Cheap and consistent, but usually not efficient.
  • Maximum likelihood: with likelihood $L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} f(X_i; \theta)$ and log-likelihood $\ell(\theta) = \sum_i \log f(X_i; \theta)$, the MLE maximizes $\ell$. Solve the score equation $$u(\theta) = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta} = 0,$$ checking $\partial^2 \ell / \partial \theta^2 \lt 0$ for a maximum. MLEs are invariant: $\widehat{g(\theta)} = g(\hat\theta)$.

Evaluating an estimator

Quality is measured by how far $\hat\theta$ lands from $\theta$ on average: $$\operatorname{Bias}(\hat\theta) = E_\theta[\hat\theta] - \theta, \qquad \operatorname{MSE}(\hat\theta) = E_\theta\!\left[(\hat\theta - \theta)^2\right] = \operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta) + \operatorname{Bias}(\hat\theta)^2.$$ This is the bias–variance decomposition: unbiasedness is not free, and a slightly biased estimator with much smaller variance can win on MSE.

  • Consistency: $\hat\theta_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta$ as $n \to \infty$— the estimator concentrates on the truth. Sufficient conditions: bias $\to 0$ and variance $\to 0$.
  • Sufficiency: $T(X)$ is sufficient for $\theta$ if the conditional distribution of the data given $T$ is free of $\theta$. Factorization criterion: $f(x; \theta) = g(T(x); \theta)\, h(x)$. Rao–Blackwell says conditioning any unbiased estimator on a sufficient statistic never increases variance.
  • Efficiency: among unbiased estimators, smaller variance is better; the floor is set by the information bound below.

Fisher information and the Cramér–Rao bound

The Fisher information measures how sharply the likelihood identifies $\theta$: $$I(\theta) = E_\theta\!\left[\left(\frac{\partial \log f}{\partial \theta}\right)^{\!2}\right] = -E_\theta\!\left[\frac{\partial^2 \log f}{\partial \theta^2}\right],$$ and information adds over an i.i.d. sample: $I_n(\theta) = n\, I(\theta)$. Any unbiased estimator obeys the Cramér–Rao lower bound $$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta) \ge \frac{1}{n\, I(\theta)}.$$ An estimator attaining it is efficient. Under regularity the MLE is asymptotically efficient and normal: $$\sqrt{n}\,(\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} N\!\bigl(0,\ I(\theta)^{-1}\bigr).$$

Sampling distributions

Interval and test procedures are built from the distribution of statistics under repeated sampling. For an i.i.d. Normal sample $X_i \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$:

  • Sample mean: $\bar X \sim N\!\bigl(\mu,\ \sigma^2/n\bigr)$; the standard error is $\operatorname{SE}(\bar X) = \sigma/\sqrt{n}$ (estimated by $s/\sqrt{n}$).
  • Sample variance: $s^2 = \dfrac{1}{n-1}\sum_i (X_i - \bar X)^2$ is unbiased for $\sigma^2$, and $\dfrac{(n-1)s^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$, independent of $\bar X$.
  • t: $\dfrac{\bar X - \mu}{s/\sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1}$—replacing $\sigma$ by $s$ inflates the tails, giving Student's $t$.
  • F: a ratio of two independent scaled chi-squares, $F = \dfrac{\chi^2_{d_1}/d_1}{\chi^2_{d_2}/d_2} \sim F_{d_1, d_2}$—the workhorse for comparing variances and nested models.

Away from Normality, the CLT and Slutsky's theorem give the large-sample versions: $\dfrac{\bar X - \mu}{s/\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} N(0, 1)$, which is why $z$- and $t$-based procedures are so widely reusable.

Uncertainty quantification

A $100(1-\alpha)\%$ confidence interval is a random interval $[L(X), U(X)]$ with $P_\theta(L \le \theta \le U) = 1 - \alpha$ for all $\theta$. The coverage statement is about the procedure, not a single realized interval.

  • Pivotal method: find a pivot $Q(X, \theta)$ whose distribution is free of $\theta$, then invert. The Normal-mean pivot $\dfrac{\bar X - \mu}{s/\sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1}$ gives $$\bar X \pm t_{n-1,\, 1-\alpha/2}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}.$$
  • Large-sample (Wald): $\hat\theta \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\, \operatorname{SE}(\hat\theta)$, using $\operatorname{SE}(\hat\theta) \approx 1/\sqrt{n\, I(\hat\theta)}$ from the MLE's asymptotic normality.
  • Delta method: to get a CI for $g(\theta)$, propagate variance through a smooth transform: $$\operatorname{Var}\bigl(g(\hat\theta)\bigr) \approx g'(\theta)^2\, \operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta),$$ so $\sqrt{n}\,(g(\hat\theta) - g(\theta)) \xrightarrow{d} N\!\bigl(0,\ g'(\theta)^2 / I(\theta)\bigr)$.
  • Bootstrap: when no clean pivot exists, resample the data (or simulate from $\hat\theta$) $B$ times, recompute $\hat\theta^{*}$, and read percentiles of the $\hat\theta^{*}$ distribution to form an interval—no closed-form SE required.

Hypothesis testing

A test partitions the sample space into a rejection region for $H_0: \theta \in \Theta_0$ versus $H_1: \theta \in \Theta_1$. Two error types trade off:

  • Type I (reject a true $H_0$): rate $\alpha = P_{H_0}(\text{reject})$, the significance level.
  • Type II (fail to reject a false $H_0$): rate $\beta$; the power is $1 - \beta = P_{H_1}(\text{reject})$, increasing in effect size, sample size, and $\alpha$.
  • p-value: the probability, under $H_0$, of a statistic at least as extreme as observed. Reject when $p \le \alpha$; read it alongside effect size, not as $P(H_0 \mid \text{data})$.

Neyman–Pearson lemma: for simple $H_0: \theta = \theta_0$ vs $H_1: \theta = \theta_1$, the most powerful level-$\alpha$ test rejects for large values of the likelihood ratio $$\Lambda = \frac{L(\theta_1)}{L(\theta_0)}.$$ For composite hypotheses the generalized likelihood ratio test uses $$\lambda = \frac{\sup_{\theta \in \Theta_0} L(\theta)}{\sup_{\theta \in \Theta} L(\theta)}, \qquad -2\log\lambda \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2_{df},$$ with $df$ = the number of free parameters constrained under $H_0$ (Wilks' theorem).

The three asymptotically equivalent tests of $H_0: \theta = \theta_0$—Wald $\dfrac{\hat\theta - \theta_0}{\operatorname{SE}(\hat\theta)}$, score $\dfrac{u(\theta_0)}{\sqrt{I(\theta_0)}}$, and the likelihood ratio $-2\log\lambda$—use, respectively, the curvature at $\hat\theta$, the slope at $\theta_0$, and the height difference of $\ell$. Duality: the set of $\theta_0$ not rejected at level $\alpha$ is exactly a $100(1-\alpha)\%$ confidence interval.

  • One-sample $t$: $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$ via $t = (\bar X - \mu_0)/(s/\sqrt n) \sim t_{n-1}$.
  • Two-sample $t$: compare means with pooled or Welch standard errors.
  • $\chi^2$: goodness-of-fit and independence in contingency tables (see Categorical data).
  • Multiplicity: testing many hypotheses inflates false positives—control the family-wise error (Bonferroni) or the false discovery rate (Benjamini–Hochberg).

Bayesian inference

Bayesian inference treats $\theta$ as random and updates a prior with the likelihood: $$p(\theta \mid x) = \frac{f(x \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)}{\int f(x \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)\, d\theta} \;\propto\; f(x \mid \theta)\, p(\theta).$$ The full posterior $p(\theta \mid x)$ is the object of inference; summaries include the posterior mean/median and the MAP estimate $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_\theta p(\theta \mid x)$.

  • Conjugacy: a prior is conjugate when the posterior stays in the same family—Beta–Binomial, Gamma–Poisson, Normal–Normal—turning updating into arithmetic on hyperparameters.
  • Credible interval: a set with $P(\theta \in C \mid x) = 1 - \alpha$; unlike a confidence interval, this is a direct probability statement about $\theta$.
  • Frequentist link: with a flat prior the MAP coincides with the MLE, and as $n \to \infty$ the posterior concentrates and looks Normal around $\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}}$ (Bernstein–von Mises)—the prior washes out.
