Statistical models & methods

A model is a deliberate simplification: it names variables, relationships, and error structure so inference and prediction have a target. Methods are the algorithms and diagnostics that fit the model to data responsibly.

The linear model

The linear regression model writes the response as a linear combination of predictors plus Gaussian noise: $$y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \cdots + \beta_p x_{ip} + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} N(0, \sigma^2),$$ or in matrix form $y = X\beta + \varepsilon$ with $X$ the $n \times (p+1)$ design matrix. The Gauss–Markov assumptions are linearity, $E[\varepsilon] = 0$, constant variance (homoscedasticity), and uncorrelated errors.

Ordinary least squares minimizes $\sum_i (y_i - x_i^\top\beta)^2 = \lVert y - X\beta\rVert^2$, giving the normal equations $X^\top X \hat\beta = X^\top y$ and $$\hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\hat\beta) = \sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}.$$ The fitted values are $\hat y = H y$ with the hat (projection) matrix $H = X(X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top$, and $\hat\sigma^2 = \dfrac{\lVert y - \hat y\rVert^2}{n - p - 1}$ is unbiased for $\sigma^2$.

  • Gauss–Markov theorem: among all linear unbiased estimators, OLS has the smallest variance—it is BLUE. Under Normal errors it is also the MLE and fully efficient.
  • Inference: $\dfrac{\hat\beta_j - \beta_j}{\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_j)} \sim t_{n-p-1}$ gives per-coefficient tests and CIs; the overall $F$-test $F = \dfrac{(\text{SSR}/p)}{(\text{SSE}/(n-p-1))} \sim F_{p,\, n-p-1}$ tests whether any predictor matters.
  • Fit: $R^2 = 1 - \text{SSE}/\text{SST}$ is the fraction of variance explained; adjusted $R^2$ penalizes added predictors.
  • Diagnostics: residual-vs-fitted plots (linearity, constant variance), Q–Q plots (Normality), leverage $h_{ii}$ and Cook's distance (influence), and variance inflation factors (multicollinearity) keep the linear story honest.

ANOVA as a linear model

Analysis of variance is regression on categorical predictors coded as indicators. One-way ANOVA with groups $1, \ldots, k$ decomposes the total sum of squares into between- and within-group parts: $$\underbrace{\sum_{ij}(y_{ij} - \bar y)^2}_{\text{SST}} = \underbrace{\sum_i n_i (\bar y_i - \bar y)^2}_{\text{SSB}} + \underbrace{\sum_{ij}(y_{ij} - \bar y_i)^2}_{\text{SSW}}.$$ The $F$-statistic $F = \dfrac{\text{SSB}/(k-1)}{\text{SSW}/(n-k)} \sim F_{k-1,\, n-k}$ tests equality of group means $H_0: \mu_1 = \cdots = \mu_k$. Two-way ANOVA adds a second factor and interaction terms; post-hoc procedures (Tukey HSD) control error across pairwise comparisons.

Generalized linear models

When the response is a count, proportion, or category, GLMs keep the linear predictor $\eta_i = x_i^\top\beta$ but connect it to the mean through a link $g$, with variance set by an exponential-family distribution: $$g(\mu_i) = x_i^\top\beta, \qquad \mu_i = E[Y_i].$$ Logistic regression (logit link, Bernoulli/Binomial) and Poisson regression (log link, counts) are the canonical cases; fitting is by maximum likelihood via iteratively reweighted least squares, and Wald/score/LR tests carry over. The Categorical data & GLMs page develops this toolkit in full.

Regularization

When $p$ is large or predictors are collinear, OLS variance explodes. Penalized regression shrinks coefficients toward zero, trading a little bias for a large variance reduction: $$\hat\beta_{\text{ridge}} = \arg\min_\beta \Bigl\{\lVert y - X\beta\rVert^2 + \lambda \sum_j \beta_j^2\Bigr\}, \qquad \hat\beta_{\text{lasso}} = \arg\min_\beta \Bigl\{\lVert y - X\beta\rVert^2 + \lambda \sum_j |\beta_j|\Bigr\}.$$

  • Ridge ($L_2$): closed form $\hat\beta = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1} X^\top y$; shrinks all coefficients smoothly, stabilizing collinear designs, but keeps every predictor.
  • Lasso ($L_1$): the corner of the constraint region drives some coefficients exactly to zero, doing selection and estimation at once.
  • Elastic net: mixes both penalties to select among correlated groups of predictors. The tuning parameter $\lambda$ is chosen by cross-validation.

Multilevel and hierarchical structure

When observations cluster—students in schools, repeated measures on people—the errors within a cluster are correlated, and a mixed model adds a random effect per group: $$y_{ij} = x_{ij}^\top\beta + u_j + \varepsilon_{ij}, \qquad u_j \sim N(0, \tau^2), \quad \varepsilon_{ij} \sim N(0, \sigma^2).$$ The group intercept $u_j$ induces intraclass correlation $\rho = \tau^2 / (\tau^2 + \sigma^2)$. Estimating $u_j$ as a compromise between the group mean and the global mean is partial pooling: it shares strength across groups, shrinking noisy small-group estimates toward the overall fit and avoiding overfitting idiosyncratic clusters.

Modern statistical learning

Flexible methods reduce bias at the cost of variance and interpretability. Expected prediction error decomposes the same way estimators do: $$E\!\left[(y - \hat f(x))^2\right] = \underbrace{\bigl(E[\hat f(x)] - f(x)\bigr)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(\hat f(x))}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible}}.$$ Choosing model complexity is choosing where to sit on this tradeoff.

  • Cross-validation: $k$-fold CV estimates out-of-sample error by rotating which fold is held out, giving an honest target for tuning complexity and comparing models.
  • Trees: recursive binary splits partition predictor space into piecewise-constant regions—interpretable but high-variance.
  • Ensembles: bagging and random forests average many decorrelated trees to cut variance; boosting (gradient boosting) fits trees sequentially to residuals to cut bias.
  • Validation that travels: calibration and external validation on fresh data translate in-sample fit into claims that generalize to new environments.
